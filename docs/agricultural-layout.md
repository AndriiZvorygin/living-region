# Agricultural layout, soil and nutrient retention

The spatial model is a management-intensity gradient. It is a design framework, not a claim that every site should use identical distances, species, or areas.

1. Residence / dwelling cluster
2. Greenhouse and intensive gardens
3. Annual food/staple fields
4. Perennial food-production zone
5. Edible shrubs and wood coppice
6. Dwarf/intermediate fruit and nut trees
7. Full-size canopy / staple trees
8. Thorny protective perimeter

## Functional logic

The greenhouse is nearest the residence because it requires frequent temperature, watering, ventilation, seedling, and harvest checks. Annual fields carry high-attention calorie and staple production and can be rotated. Perennial production supplies long-lived food, continuous roots, and soil cover. Shrubs and coppice supply food, poles, mulch, biomass, and renewable heating fuel. Dwarf/intermediate trees add fruit and nut production without immediately creating the management and shade regime of a mature canopy.

Potential canopy functions are kept separate from suitability claims: chestnut may serve as a starch crop; walnut or heartnut as fat/protein-bearing nuts; white oak as an acorn staple; and honey locust as mast/fodder or seed production where a suitable cultivar and site support it. The food-energy model does not establish that any species is suitable for Grey-Bruce, safe, productive at the cited yield, or nutritionally adequate.

The outer edge uses thorny trees, shrubs, and perennial vegetation as a design objective to reduce deer/wildlife entry, intercept runoff, retain sediment and nutrients, and shelter inner production zones. Candidate species mentioned in the historical material include honey locust, Korean pine, osage orange, seabuckthorn, hawthorn, and raspberry; these are not all mathematically or ecologically interchangeable.

## Soil, water and nutrient objective

The intended pattern is annual production embedded inside progressively more perennial vegetation. Runoff leaving disturbed inner zones should pass through successive living-root and perennial layers before reaching the boundary. This can:

- reduce off-site sediment loss and erosion;
- reduce nutrient export;
- slow and capture runoff;
- maintain continuous roots and soil organic matter;
- provide wind protection;
- recycle biomass and nutrients locally.

The objective is to minimize loss, not to promise zero runoff. Actual performance depends on slope, soil texture, drainage, rainfall intensity, compaction, ground cover, swales or other earthworks, livestock pressure, and maintenance. Any future erosion-rate analysis must distinguish regional averages, severe-site examples, and estimated soil-formation rates rather than presenting one severe rate as a North American average.

## Relationship to the energy model

Food-energy land and coppice land are the explicit arithmetic streams. Perennial buffers, nutrient interception, runoff control, wildlife protection, fibre/materials, soil-building rotations, and unproductive establishment years are resilience/design allowances. They should be displayed as such in a future ARC interface rather than hidden inside a single productivity coefficient.
